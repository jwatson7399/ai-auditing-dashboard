Lead · United States · Nov 16

US to require licences for AI chips at data centres serving Chinese firms

The Commerce Department will require licences for AI chips sent to any data centre that gives large-scale computing to firms headquartered in China, Macau or other US arms-embargoed countries, and it ends the AI Diffusion Rule.

Why it matters: data-centre operators abroad must check where their customers’ parent companies are based.

Source: Commerce (BIS) release · Rule text (PDF) · Federal Register inspection copy

Details

Confirmed (interim final rule RIN 0694-AK50, released Nov 16; Federal Register document 2026-23529, on public inspection Nov 16 and scheduled for publication Nov 19; effective on publication; comments due 60 days later).

Who it covers: data centres giving access to more than 12,000,000 “aggregate TPP” (a measure of chip computing power), at one site or through linked sites, to firms headquartered in, or whose ultimate parent is in, Macau or Country Group D:5. Commercial recommender systems are excepted.

Five countries: chips sent to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil and Thailand also need a licence; it does not apply to users headquartered in the US that meet reporting requirements.

The Bureau of Industry and Security says the rule was written “by and at the direction of” the Treasury and the National Security Council. Singapore’s digital and trade ministries say they are studying the rule and engaging US authorities.

Co‑lead · New York · Nov 17

New York’s attorney general sets up a unit to enforce AI safety laws

Attorney General Letitia James created an AI Safety, Standards, Enforcement, and Transparency (ASSET) Unit to enforce the RAISE Act and other AI safety laws.

Why it matters: AI developers get a named state enforcer before the RAISE Act takes effect on Jan 1, 2027.

Source: NY Attorney General release

Details

Confirmed (official release, Nov 17). Cristy Phillips leads the unit inside the Economic Justice Division, working with the Bureau of Internet and Technology. It will also draft policy proposals and support investigations. The team is still being built; no budget or staff count was given.

Date conflict: the release says the children’s chatbot law (S.9051B/A.10379C) takes effect “in June 2027”, but the bill as passed says Jan 1, 2027. No later amendment was found, so which date applies is unclear.

Also · United Kingdom · Nov 16

UK regulators want early contact before personal AI agents launch

The UK’s competition, financial, data and communications regulators told ministers that firms launching personal AI agents should engage them early and meet legal duties before launch.

Why it matters: partners such as banks keep their own duties, and the regulators say they will act on suspected breaches.

Source: Joint letter (PDF)

Details

Confirmed (letter dated Nov 16, sent through the Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum and signed by the heads of the CMA, FCA, ICO and Ofcom). It cites the “forthcoming launch of a number of personal agentic products in the UK” and names fraud and scams, sharing personal information “not in line with user expectations” and misleading recommendations as risks. It says engagement “has not been consistently early or open”. This sets expectations; it is not a rule or formal guidance.

Also · Ireland · Nov 17

X paused AI training on data its agent collected after Ireland’s regulator asked

Ireland’s data regulator asked X to stop training on others’ data collected by its Grok Bot agent, and X confirmed it had, a commissioner told Politico.

Why it matters: it was a request, not a formal order.

Source: Politico

Details

Outlet reports (Politico, Nov 17). Commissioner Dale Sunderland: “We asked them to suspend the processing, and they’ve confirmed to us today that they’ve done that.” He said this on Monday, Nov 16. Politico says the data collection began in late October. He said the Data Protection Commission is talking to another, unnamed firm about the same practice. X did not respond to Politico, and the Commission has posted no statement of its own.

Also · Nvidia · Nov 17

Nvidia stops shipping its US-licensed Hopper chips to China

Nvidia’s interim CEO said it is “no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China”, after $2.4 billion of those sales last quarter.

Why it matters: its forecast assumes no China data-centre computing revenue; no reason was given.

Source: Nvidia results release · Call transcript (third-party copy)

Details

Confirmed. The results release gives a fourth-quarter revenue outlook of $130.0 billion, plus or minus 3%, and says Nvidia “is not assuming any Data Center compute revenue from China”. Interim CEO Colette Kress made the Hopper remarks on the Nov 17 earnings call; they and the $2.4 billion figure are not in the release; Reuters reports the same wording. Nvidia did not say whether the halt is permanent.
